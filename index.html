The thesis

For as long as human beings have possessed consciousness, they have confronted a recurring problem: the first version of something is rarely the final form it deserves.

Civilizations revise their laws. Architects renovate buildings. Musicians remaster records. Engineers issue second revisions. Software receives patches. Fashion cycles refine silhouettes. Idols discover which hairstyle should obviously have been used from the beginning. Car manufacturers look at a perfectly serviceable front bumper and eventually admit, after several model years of pretending otherwise, that the headlights were indeed slightly wrong.

Human history may therefore be understood as one continuous struggle between two forces.

ZENKI

Here is the thing as we initially made it.

KOUKI

We have now had time to look at the thing.

This distinction is more profound than mere chronology. It represents two competing theories of existence.

Zenki asks us to respect intention.
Kouki asks us to respect results.

And results win.

The thesis of this essay is therefore uncompromising: Kouki > Zenki.

Not merely as a preference. Not merely in cars. Not merely in K-pop styling. Not merely because somebody's friend has developed an emotional attachment to the earlier version and now needs to construct increasingly elaborate arguments about “purity.”

Rather, kouki superiority emerges independently from virtually every system of thought humanity has developed.

To reject kouki is not simply to disagree about aesthetics.

It is to place oneself in opposition to the arrow of civilization.

The Psychological Argument

Why, then, do intelligent people continue preferring zenki?

Psychology provides several explanations.

1. Mere-exposure effect

The first version encountered becomes mentally normalized. Later changes therefore feel incorrect not because they are worse, but because familiarity has been disturbed.

2. Status-quo bias

People systematically overvalue existing arrangements. Thus “the old lights looked better” may frequently mean “the new lights do not match the mental image I spent five years developing.”

3. Nostalgia

Human memory compresses inconvenience and preserves emotional atmosphere.

Zenki becomes associated with youth, discovery, magazine covers, old games, childhood, the first time someone saw the car.

Kouki must compete not against an object, but against autobiographical memory.

This is deeply unfair.

4. Scarcity rationalization

If somebody owns or can afford zenki but not kouki, the mind may begin producing emergency philosophical literature about why the earlier one was secretly better anyway.
